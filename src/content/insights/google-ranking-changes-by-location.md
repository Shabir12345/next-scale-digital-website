Ask a shop owner where they rank and you usually get one number. Ask where that
number came from and it is almost always the same story: someone searched the
keyword, saw the business at position three, and wrote down three.

That number is close to useless, and acting on it wastes money.

## The same search returns different results to different people

Google personalises local results by where the searcher is standing. Proximity is
one of the three factors it uses to build the map pack, alongside relevance and
prominence. When someone in Vaughan searches for a service, Google assembles a
candidate list weighted toward businesses near Vaughan. Someone running the
identical search in Scarborough gets a different list.

This is not a glitch or a personalisation setting anyone can switch off. It is
the core of how local search works, and it is the right behaviour: a person
looking for a tow truck wants one that can reach them.

The consequence for a business is that "our ranking" is not a single value. It is
a surface. A shop can hold the top map slot for searches within two kilometres,
sit at position seven at five kilometres, and be absent entirely at ten.

## What a single rank check actually measures

When a rank tracker reports one position for one keyword, it checked from one
location. Usually that location is the geographic centre of the city, chosen
because it is the obvious default.

For a business at the centre of the city, that reading is flattering and
misleading in your favour. For a business at the edge of a service area, it is
brutal and misleading against you. Neither one tells you what a customer sees.

We audited a tire shop earlier this year whose city-centre check placed it
second on its main term. Measured properly across its service area, it held a
top-three position at one point out of five. The centre reading was not wrong
about that one location. It was wrong about the business.

## Measuring the surface instead of the point

The fix is to check the same keyword from a grid of points spread across the area
you actually serve, and report all of them.

The output is a map rather than a number: green where you hold the pack, amber
where you are on page one but below the fold, red where you are effectively
invisible. Twenty-five points across a service area gives you a picture you can
act on. One point gives you a number you can only argue about.

What that picture tells you:

- **Where you already win.** These are the areas to defend and to name in your
  service pages, because you are converting there already.
- **Where you are close.** Amber points a few positions off the pack are the
  cheapest wins available. Small movements in profile quality and reviews
  frequently flip them.
- **Where you should stop spending.** If you are absent from an area, and there
  are four established competitors physically inside it, no amount of content
  fixes that. Proximity is not a factor you can buy your way past. Better to
  accept it and spend the budget where you are already close.

## Why this matters more than the website

Most of the levers that move the grid are not on your site.

The Google Business Profile carries the categories, the service list, the service
area, the hours and the photos. Those decide what you are eligible to appear for.
Reviews decide prominence. Name, address and phone consistency across the rest of
the internet decides whether Google is confident it is looking at one business
rather than three.

A business can have an excellent website and be nearly invisible on the map
because the profile has one category and eleven reviews. The reverse is also
common, and cheaper to fix.

## What to do with this

If you are paying for local SEO and the report shows one position per keyword, ask
for the grid. If the answer is that they do not measure that way, you are being
reported to on a metric that does not describe your business.

before you do. It tells you whether your problem is visibility, conversion, or
something else entirely, and those three have completely different price tags.
